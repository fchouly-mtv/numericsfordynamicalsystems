\section{Introduction (more or less autobiographic)}

I write this note as a kind of entry point to the wonderful universe of numerical analysis related to dynamical systems in a broad sense, with emphasis on ordinary differential equations with geometric properties and the calculation of bifurcations. These notes are motivated by a seminar on this topic I am organizing in 2026 at the Udelar (Montevideo, Uruguay) for undergraduate and graduate students (in the framework of the Pedeciba program for graduate and doctoral studies). They are also inspired by a lot of discussions I had, by some talks I attended, and by reading some books.

\medskip

This story started very early with discussions on fractals and chaos I had with some close friends % Francois Barbut, Sophie Cahuzac
but a turning point was some talks organised in Grenoble, France, where I did my engineering and doctoral studies (2000--2006). Particularly I remember two talks by Jacques Demongeot about dynamical systems in biology, that  were very inspiring, and an excellent talk by Yves Colin de Verdi\`ere on ergodicity. In parallel, I started reading some books by Benoit Mandelbrot about fractals~\cite{mandelbrot} and I had a very nice discussion with Jacques Viret (TIMC Laboratory, Grenoble), who was starting to write his book about topology and psychology~\cite{viret}. He even had the kindness to give me a first draft of this book for revision. Jacques Demongeot, Jacques Viret and other mathematicians from TIMC were directly inspired by some works by Ren\'e Thom.
I also had discussions on dynamical systems, theoretical biology and complexity with other colleagues, notably Loic Forest and Nicolas Glade.

\medskip

Later on, in 2008--2009, when I was doing a postdoc in Paris, under the supervision of Miguel Fern\'andez, I worked on time-parallel time-integration for coupled partial differential equations (it was motivated by fluid-structure interactions in blood flows). The methods we studied underwent stability problems for conservative systems, like elastodynamics, and more generally for hamiltonian systems with symplectic geometric structure. This allowed me to discover many issues about numerical ordinary differential equations, the works of Martin Gander, Yvon Maday and other mathematicians, and the wonderful books by Ernst Hairer and colleagues. After two years of postdoc, I found no solution for this problem (I am still active on this issue in my free hours, and I still try some ideas with little success).

\medskip

Around 2016, Yves Renard and I were invited to publish a paper~\cite{chouly2018explicit} for a special issue of the journal \emph{Advanced Modeling and Simulation in Engineering Sciences}, on the topic \emph{Towards new paradigms for time integrators in computational dynamics}. This allowed me to study in more depth the St\"ormer--Verlet scheme and how it can be applied to contact in elastodynamics. I also had the opportunity to discuss with Laurent Monasse (at the WCCM 2016 in New York) and Fr\'ed\'eric Marazzato (I invited him for a talk in Dijon later on) about the new explicit time-marching scheme with pseudo-hamiltonian structure that they designed with Christian Mariotti and Alexandre Ern~\cite{marazzato2019}.

\medskip

Around the year 2020, I was invited by Kanty Rabenorosoa to co-supervise Emerson Takematsu, a master student from Brazil, on modelling and simulation for soft robotics. It involved mathematical models of thin structures (rods) and bifurcation analysis. I had some interactions with Florence Bertails and S\'ebastien Neukirch who are outstanding contributors to the topic, see for instance their joint work~\cite{bertails2025noether}, and the work of S\'ebastien Neukirch with Basile Audoly, about spaghettis~\cite{audoly2005spaghetti} (Ig Nobel Prize 2006). They suggested I have a look at the book by R. Seydel to start understanding the topic of numerical bifurcations. 

\medskip

I had also some nice discussions with
Christian Bonatti in the coffee room of the IMB (Dijon), particularly about the Brouwer fixed point theorem, and I met a lot of Latin American students he worked with.
%
Before leaving Dijon, I had the first opportunity to give lectures there on numerical ordinary differential equations, to students of the international master of mathematical physics~\cite{chouly:hal-03212748}.

\medskip

Now, and since 2024, in Montevideo, I have the chance to hear some talks by colleagues from the dynamical systems group, particularly by Sof\'ia Llavayol, Roberto Markarian, Alessandro Passeggi and Raphael Potrie, and also to have some discussions with Fran\c{c}oise Dal'Bo, Alessandro Passeggi, Sof\'ia Llavayol and Mauricio Velasco (see for instance
\cite{dalbo2026ifumi} and \cite{markarian2025dimension} for the general context). Most of the discussions are about the practical detection and characterization of chaos, due to recent breakthrough from Alessandro and his colleagues~\cite{capi,passeggi2026conditions}.
This inspired a research project (PREXI Pedeciba) that I carried out with Guillermo Bustos, a talented undergraduate student who was under my supervision in 2025. 
On the other hand, I had the chance to start other related activities, such as lectures on numerical differential equations (with a related book that has been published by the Dirac editions), discussions with Marcelo Forets, Jorge Perez and Juan Pablo Borthagaray about reachability analysis and discussions with Benjamin Georgette and C\'edric Pozzolini about Poisson's structures for continuum thermomechanics.

\section{Basic references}

The two main references of the seminar are the following.

\subsection{Hairer, Lubich and Wanner}

The monograph of
Hairer, Lubich and Wanner~\cite{hairer2006}, first published in
2002 is by now a reference on geometric numerical
integration. The book develops the modern theory of
structure-preserving time integrators for ordinary differential
equations: rather than viewing a numerical scheme merely as an
approximation tool with a given order of accuracy, the authors
promote the viewpoint that a good integrator should inherit the
geometric structure of the flow it approximates. This leads to a
systematic study of symplectic methods for Hamiltonian systems.

% -- whose central tool is backward error analysis, showing that a
% symplectic integrator is the exact flow of a modified
% Hamiltonian over exponentially long time intervals -- as well as
% of symmetric methods for reversible systems,
% variational integrators, methods preserving first integrals and
% Lyapunov functions, and splitting and composition techniques
% that build complex schemes from elementary flows. Beyond the
% construction of methods, the book provides the analytical
% machinery needed to assess their long-time behaviour: modified
% equations, Hamiltonian and reversible perturbation theory,
% KAM-type results for numerical flows, and linear and nonlinear
% stability analysis, always with an eye on the energy behaviour
% over long time spans. Extensive numerical experiments -- the
% famous ``folklore'' observations on the energy drift of
% non-symplectic versus symplectic schemes -- illustrate the
% theory and make the book simultaneously a research monograph
% and a practical guide. 

\subsection{Seydel}

A possible entry point to the numerical side of bifurcation
analysis is the book of Seydel~\cite{seydel2010}, whose third
edition summarises three decades of development of the field.
The central object of the book is the computation of
stationary solutions and their stability, and the systematic
numerical detection of the bifurcations through which solution
branches lose or exchange stability: turning points,
branch points, Hopf bifurcations and the routes they open
towards periodic orbits and chaotic regimes. The distinctive
feature of the approach is that bifurcation analysis is
formulated as a numerical continuation problem: solution
branches are traced by combining Newton-type solvers with
predictor--corrector path-following strategies, and
bifurcation points are detected and located as special
solutions of augmented systems.

\subsection{Time integration for Hamiltonian dynamics} %and
%structure-preserving methods}

Many physical systems -- planetary motion, elasticity, wave
phenomena -- are described by a function $H(q,p)$ of positions
$q$ and momenta $p$, called the \emph{Hamiltonian}, and evolve
according to Hamilton's equations
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hamilton}
  \dot q = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p}(q,p),
  \qquad
  \dot p = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q}(q,p).
\end{equation}
This seemingly simple structure has a remarkable consequence: the
flow of~\eqref{eq:hamilton} preserves the total energy
$H(q(t),p(t))$ and the volume in phase space. A natural question
is whether a numerical scheme can inherit these properties.
The answer is generally no for classical schemes: for instance,
the explicit Euler method applied to a harmonic oscillator
($H=\tfrac12(p^2+q^2)$) produces a discrete orbit spiralling
outwards, so the computed energy grows without bound. In
contrast, the \emph{symplectic Euler} method, which updates $p$
before $q$ using the fresh value,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:symplectic-euler}
  p_{n+1} = p_n - h\,\frac{\partial H}{\partial q}(q_n,p_{n+1}),
  \qquad
  q_{n+1} = q_n + h\,\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}(q_n,p_{n+1}),
\end{equation}
preserves the symplectic structure of the flow and, by backward
error analysis, behaves over exponentially long times like the
exact flow of a slightly perturbed Hamiltonian. This is the
central theme of~\cite{hairer2006} and the guiding principle of
structure-preserving integration: \emph{the discretization
should respect the geometry of the equation}.

\subsection{Numerical integration on Lie groups}

Some systems are constrained to live on a curved set carrying a
group structure -- rotations of a rigid body, unit quaternions
for orientation, orthogonal matrices -- collectively called Lie
groups. A numerical method that treats the coordinates as if
they lived in a flat space $\R^d$ produces iterates that leave
the group, destroying the invariants of the problem. The remedy
is to express the motion \emph{through} the group using the
exponential map. If $G$ is a matrix group and
$\mathfrak g = T_eG$ its tangent space at the identity, the
flow of a vector field on $G$ can be advanced by solving an
equation in the tangent space and mapping the result back:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exp}
  y(t) = \exp\bigl(\xi(t)\bigr)\,y(0),
  \qquad \xi(t)\in\mathfrak g,
\end{equation}
where $\exp$ is the matrix exponential. This is the principle
of \emph{Lie group methods}: the update of $\xi$ is computed by
a classical scheme, while the exponential guarantees that
$y(t)$ remains on $G$ at every step.

\subsection{Reachability analysis}\label{sec:reachability}

The last decade has seen the rise of a complementary question:
rather than computing or simulating individual trajectories,
one asks what is the \emph{set of all possible states} that a
system can attain. This is the subject of \emph{reachability
analysis}. The motivation is twofold. First, many systems are
subject to uncertainty: uncertain initial conditions, uncertain
parameters, or inputs chosen by an external agent. Second,
many applications -- verification of safety properties,
control synthesis, air-traffic management, robotics -- require
guarantees that hold for \emph{all} trajectories at once, not
merely for simulated ones. A typical safety question reads:
\emph{is it true that every trajectory starting in the initial
set $X_0$ avoids the unsafe set $U$ for all times $t\in[0,T]$?}

The mathematical formulation can be as follows. Given a discrete
dynamics $x_{n+1}=\varphi(x_n)$ and a set of initial states
$X_0$, the \emph{reachable set} after $N$ steps is defined
recursively by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reach}
  \mathcal R_0 = X_0,
  \qquad
  \mathcal R_{n+1} = \varphi(\mathcal R_n)
  := \{\varphi(x) : x\in\mathcal R_n\},
\end{equation}
and the safety question becomes
$\mathcal R_n \cap U = \varnothing$ for all $n\le N$.

\subsection{Numerical computation of bifurcations}

When a differential equation depends on a parameter $\lambda$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:param}
  \dot x = f(x,\lambda),
  \qquad x\in\R^n,\ \lambda\in\R,
\end{equation}
the structure of its equilibrium solutions -- points $x$ with
$f(x,\lambda)=0$ -- can change qualitatively as $\lambda$
crosses special values, called \emph{bifurcation points}. The
simplest example is the one-dimensional equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:saddle-node}
  \dot x = \lambda - x^2,
\end{equation}
which has two equilibria $x=\pm\sqrt{\lambda}$ for $\lambda>0$,
one stable and one unstable, and no equilibrium at all for
$\lambda<0$: at $\lambda=0$ a pair of equilibria is born
(\emph{saddle-node bifurcation}). Numerical bifurcation
analysis aims to \emph{compute} such phenomena: one traces the
branch of equilibria with a continuation method (a
predictor--corrector algorithm driven by Newton's method),
monitors \emph{test functions} whose vanishing signals a
bifurcation, and solves augmented systems to locate the
bifurcation point precisely and switch to the emerging branch.
This is the algorithmic core of~\cite{seydel2010}. The
difficulty, and the interest of the subject, is that the
bifurcation is a global property of the branch: no local
information at a single point reveals it.
